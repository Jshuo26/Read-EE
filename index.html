Every miscue tells you something.

Read-EE listens to a learner read a passage aloud, lines up what they said against the reference text word by word using Levenshtein alignment, then runs the resulting pattern of mispronunciations, substitutions, omissions, insertions and repetitions through a decision tree to name the likely difficulty type.

Built-in sample passages are for trying the tool out. For an actual assessment, paste the passage from your reading instrument (e.g. a Phil-IRI passage) as a custom passage instead.

How the diagnosis works

Alignment. The reference passage and the recognized speech are each split into words, then aligned with a word-level Levenshtein (edit-distance) algorithm — the same technique behind spell-checkers and word-error-rate scoring — to find the minimum set of matches, substitutions, omissions and insertions that turns one sequence into the other.

Sub-classification. Each substitution is re-scored with a character-level Levenshtein distance between the two words. A spoken word that is orthographically close to the reference word (e.g. "sleap" for "sleep") is labeled a mispronunciation; a spoken word that is simply a different word is labeled a substitution. Extra words that echo a neighboring word are labeled repetitions rather than plain insertions.

Decision tree. Word-recognition accuracy is banded the way an informal reading inventory bands it — independent (≥97%), instructional (90–96%), frustration (<90%) — then cross-referenced with reading rate (words correct per minute) and whichever miscue type dominates, to output a specific difficulty type rather than only a level.

Limitations. Recognition quality depends on the browser's speech service, which is not tuned for children's or accented speech and can silently "auto-correct" a mispronunciation to the intended real word — undercounting some miscues, exactly as this project's own faster-whisper testing notes observed. Treat Read-EE as a fast screening aid, not a replacement for a trained examiner's judgment.
